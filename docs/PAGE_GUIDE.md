# Page by page guide

This guide explains every page in plain words: what it is for, who would use it, what you will see, and one thing to try. No technical background is needed. If you are using the public demo, the sample data is already loaded.

The left menu groups the pages by what you are trying to do: **Understand**, **Interpret**, **Strategize**, **Decide**, **Automate** and **Reference**.

## Understand

### Dashboard (the home page)
**What it is for:** the one page a busy executive needs. It answers "is our advertising money earning its keep?"
**Who uses it:** anyone. A switch at the top ("Viewing as") changes the page for a finance chief, a marketing chief, an agency director or a measurement specialist.
**What you will see:**
* A headline such as "$105,158 of ad spend has not been earned back", with a short summary underneath.
* A row of four confidence cards: how sure we are, how many campaigns have clearly real results, how many are ready for a decision, and how much of the spend was tested.
* A plain note when the two ways of counting results disagree.
* Six sections: The answer, The advisory council, The evidence (charts), Decisions for your sign-off, A "what if" scenario, and Sources.
**Try this:** switch "Viewing as" to CFO / Finance and look at the list of campaigns ranked by money not earned back. Then use the sidebar to change how the return is counted and watch the numbers change.

### Upload data
**What it is for:** bringing your own data in five guided steps (declare, upload, map columns, check, run).
**What you will see:** friendly checks that point to the exact rows with problems, and a rule that nothing runs until blockers are fixed.
**On the public demo:** switched off, so nobody shares company files on a public server.

### Runs
**What it is for:** a history of every analysis, so you can open an earlier one. Each run is saved with the data and settings it used.
**Try this:** open the list and note that each run carries a short reference code. That code appears on decisions so you can trace them back.

### Settings
**What it is for:** the company's own rules in one place: the profit margin (so "breakeven" is real), how strict to be about evidence, and which counting method to lead with.
**On the public demo:** read only.

## Interpret

### Advisory council
**What it is for:** four experienced advisors read the same results from different angles, so a leader sees where opinions agree and where judgement is needed.
**The advisors:** the Steward (finance, careful), the Builder (growth, optimistic), the Translator (agency, client focused) and the Mechanic (platform and measurement, practical).
**What you will see:** where the council agrees and splits, each advisor's view of each channel ("Put more behind it", "Keep as is", "Test again before deciding", "Reduce spend", "Prove it before deciding"), and what would change their mind.
**Also on this page:** your guardrails. Pick a risk appetite (Conservative, Balanced or Aggressive) and see how many campaigns each would flag before you apply it.
**Try this:** choose Conservative, read how the flagged list grows, then switch back.

## Strategize

### Strategy
**What it is for:** turning results into company decisions. If budget moved from one channel to another, what would change, and how sure are we?
**What you will see:** best and worst cases, the cost of waiting, how far a new channel's return could fall before the move stops paying, who is affected (finance, marketing, agencies, data teams, legal), and a monitor that shows whether returns are holding.
**Important:** numbers the system cannot know (costs, hours, contract terms) are left blank for you to fill in. It never makes them up.
**Try this:** open "Trade-offs" and read the best and worst case.

### AI brief
**What it is for:** writing a safe, ready to paste prompt so your company's own AI assistant can help draft a board note.
**What you will see:** a prompt built from numbered facts, a privacy scan that blocks emails, phone numbers and card numbers, and an answer checker that flags any number in the AI's reply that does not match the facts.
**Why it matters:** raw data never leaves; only summary totals are shared.

### Research next
**What it is for:** after a result, the honest question is "what should we test next?" This page ranks tests worth running (a longer test, a bigger test, a re-test around seasonality).
**What you will see:** a ranked list with computed test lengths and spend, plus two helper tools: one that finds look alike markets to compare against, and one that turns a test result into a starting belief for a more advanced model.

## Decide

### Sign-off desk
**What it is for:** the last step before anything changes. Every suggestion becomes a "decision packet" that a named person approves, changes or rejects.
**What you will see:** a queue of decisions, a plain summary of each (what is proposed, why, what is at stake, how sure we are), and a signing form with a written note, your email, your role and four confirmations.
**What happens next:** the signature goes into a permanent record in which every entry is linked to the one before, so any later edit or deletion shows up. You can download a signed receipt.
**On the public demo:** signing is simulated.
**Try this:** pick a decision, choose "Approve as suggested", fill in the form and sign. Then open the record at the bottom and see the "Log intact" check.

## Automate

### Automation blueprint
**What it is for:** shows how AI tools could one day connect to this system safely. It is a design, not a running server.
**What you will see:** the six narrow tools an AI would be allowed to use (five that only read and one that proposes), the tools it is never given (approve and execute), the safety rules, the weekly rhythm and a rollout plan.

## Reference

### Memos
Drafts short executive memos from verified facts. Every number in a memo is checked against the facts, and a memo with an unmatched number is rejected.

### Benchmarks
The outside reference numbers used for context, each with its source, date and confidence grade. Claims that could not be verified are listed with the reason, so you can see what was left out and why.

### How it works
The methodology in plain language: how the controlled test works, what the confidence levels mean, how breakeven is worked out, what the system will never do, and its honest limits.
